529 Plan vs Roth IRA for Kids: College Savings Showdown

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 5, 2026 · Updated November 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Parents and grandparents who want to save for a child's future often compare a 529 college savings plan with a custodial Roth IRA. Both offer tax-advantaged growth, but they differ in what the money can pay for, how financial aid treats them, and who controls the account. Neither is universally better, and many families use one, the other, or both.

What a 529 plan is

A 529 plan is a state-sponsored savings account that grows tax-free when used for qualified education expenses. Qualified expenses include tuition, required fees, books, and certain room and board costs at eligible schools, and many plans also cover some K–12 tuition. Contributions are generally not deductible at the federal level, though some states offer a state income tax deduction or credit, often for contributions to that state's own plan.

What a custodial Roth IRA is

A custodial Roth IRA is an individual retirement account opened for a minor, managed by an adult custodian until the child reaches the age of majority set by their state. The child must have earned income to make contributions, and contributions are limited to the amount of that earned income, up to the annual IRS limit. Growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Contributions can usually be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, but earnings have different rules.

Flexibility and control

A 529 plan keeps control with the account owner, even after the beneficiary reaches adulthood. If the child does not attend college, the owner can change the beneficiary to another eligible family member, or withdraw the money with taxes and penalties on the earnings. A custodial Roth IRA transfers to the child at the age of majority, and the young adult then controls the money. That can be an advantage if you want the child to build retirement savings, and a concern if you are worried about how the money will be used.

Control matters: Once a custodial account transfers, the young adult decides how to use it. Think about how much control you are comfortable giving up, and when.

The 529-to-Roth rollover

Recent federal law allows certain unused 529 balances to be rolled over into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary, subject to conditions. These include that the 529 account has been open for a minimum number of years, that the rollover follows the annual Roth contribution rules, and that there is a lifetime maximum on the amount that can be moved. Recent contributions may also be excluded from eligibility. This option makes the 529 less risky if the child does not need the full balance for education, but the rules are specific, so check the current requirements with the plan administrator and a tax professional.

A simple decision guide

A 529 plan often suits families who expect education costs and want a dedicated, tax-advantaged education account with the owner in control. A custodial Roth IRA may suit families who want to build long-term retirement savings for a child with earned income, and who are comfortable with the account passing to the child at adulthood. Some families use both, with the 529 covering education and the Roth supporting retirement. The right balance depends on your goals, your state's tax rules, the child's likely path, and how much control you want to keep.

Common mistakes

  • Opening a custodial Roth without earned income to support the contribution.
  • Withdrawing 529 earnings for non-education purposes and forgetting the tax and penalty.
  • Choosing a 529 in another state without checking whether your home state offers a tax benefit for its own plan.
  • Assuming financial aid treatment without checking the current formulas.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a 529 for a child's private K–12 school?

Many plans allow limited K–12 tuition withdrawals, subject to annual caps and plan rules. Check your specific plan before withdrawing.

What happens to a custodial Roth if the child never has earned income?

Contributions generally must be supported by earned income. Without it, you may not be able to contribute, so plan around the child's work history.

Should I save in a 529 first?

Many families prioritize their own retirement and emergency savings before funding a child's education account. Consider your own financial security alongside your child's goals.
