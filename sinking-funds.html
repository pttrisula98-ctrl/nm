Sinking Funds: Plan for Irregular Expenses

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 11, 2026 · Updated November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Many expenses feel like surprises only because they do not happen every month. Car registration, a dentist visit, annual insurance premiums, holiday gifts, and property tax all arrive on their own schedule. A sinking fund solves this by setting aside a small amount each month for each known future cost, so the money is there when the bill arrives.

What a sinking fund is

A sinking fund is money saved for a specific, known expense that will occur at some point in the future. Each fund has a target, a timeline, and a monthly contribution. The name comes from the idea of a fund that "sinks" in value as money is drawn out for its purpose. In practice, it is just a dedicated savings pot with a clear job.

The key difference from an emergency fund is predictability. You know roughly what the expense will cost and when it will come. An emergency fund covers the unknown.

Step 1: List your irregular expenses

Review twelve months of bank and card statements. Look for costs that appear once or twice a year, or that vary widely from month to month. Common examples include vehicle registration and inspection, car maintenance, home repairs, property tax or homeowner's insurance if not paid through an escrow account, annual subscriptions, medical and dental costs, gifts, clothing for seasonal changes, and travel. Add anything you forgot during the review, because most people miss several categories on the first pass.

Step 2: Estimate the annual cost

For each category, estimate the total you spend in a typical year. Use past spending where possible, and round up for surprises. For a car maintenance fund, that might mean the total of oil changes, tires, and repairs over the last two years, divided by two.

Step 3: Divide by twelve

Divide each annual estimate by twelve. That is your monthly contribution. A simple example shows how the method works:

Sinking fundAnnual costMonthly contributionWhen it is used
Car registration and inspection$360$30Once a year
Car maintenance and repairs$1,200$100As needed, throughout the year
Annual software and memberships$240$20Once a year on renewal
Holiday gifts$600$50Late in the year
Dental and vision costs$480$40As needed, throughout the year
Total$2,880$240

These figures are illustrative. In this example, the household sets aside $240 each month, which means the car registration, gifts, and dental bills are covered without touching the regular budget or a credit card.

Step 4: Keep each fund separate

Holding all your sinking funds in one account makes it easy to spend the wrong money. Use separate savings sub-accounts or buckets, one for each fund, so you can see the balance for each purpose. Many banks let you name sub-accounts. Some budgeting apps offer similar categories. If you use a single account, track each fund in a spreadsheet.

Check the balances: Once a quarter, compare each fund's balance with the amount you expect to need in the next six months. If a fund is short, increase its contribution or move money from a fund with a surplus.

Step 5: Adjust as you learn

Your first estimates will be approximate. After a year of tracking, you will know whether a fund is consistently overfunded or underfunded. Adjust the monthly contribution accordingly. If a fund reaches a level well above your expected need, you can redirect the excess to another goal or to your emergency fund.

How sinking funds fit into your budget

Sinking funds belong in your budget as a regular expense category, just like rent or groceries. This matters because a sinking fund contribution that is not in the budget is easy to skip. If you use a zero-based approach, assign each contribution a dollar amount in the plan before the month begins. Common mistakes

  • Leaving out irregular expenses entirely, then treating them as emergencies.
  • Underestimating annual costs, especially for cars and home repairs.
  • Spending from one fund to cover another, which leaves both short.
  • Stopping contributions when a fund already has a balance, instead of reviewing the target.

Frequently asked questions

How many sinking funds should I have?

As many as you need to cover your real irregular expenses, but start with the largest and most predictable ones. Too many small funds can become hard to manage.

Is a sinking fund the same as an emergency fund?

No. A sinking fund covers planned, known costs. An emergency fund covers unplanned events. Keep them separate so a planned expense does not drain your protection against surprises.

Where should I keep sinking fund money?

A high-yield savings account or a separate sub-account at your bank works well. Choose a place where the money is accessible when the bill arrives.
