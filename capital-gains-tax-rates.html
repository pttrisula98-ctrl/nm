Long-Term vs Short-Term Capital Gains: Rates and Holding Periods

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 2, 2026 · Updated November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain. In the United States, how long you held the investment determines whether that gain is taxed at short-term rates, which match ordinary income, or at lower long-term rates. The holding period is one of the most important, and most easily overlooked, factors in investment tax planning.

The holding period rule

A gain is long-term if you held the asset for more than one year, and short-term if you held it for one year or less. The clock generally starts the day after you acquire the asset and ends on the day you sell it. Because of this rule, selling one day too early can change the tax rate applied to the gain, so check the purchase date before placing a sale order when the timing is close.

Short-term gains

Short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income at your federal marginal rate. For someone in a higher bracket, that can mean a substantially higher tax than the long-term rate on the same gain. Short-term gains also commonly arise from frequent trading, from selling shares bought through certain compensation arrangements shortly after acquisition, and from rebalancing in taxable accounts.

Long-term gains and the three brackets

Long-term capital gains are taxed at preferential federal rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your taxable income and filing status. The income thresholds for each rate are adjusted periodically, so check the current IRS schedules before planning a sale. The key planning point is that the rate depends on total taxable income in the year of the sale, not just the gain itself.

The 0% opportunity

In years when your taxable income is low enough, long-term gains may fall into the 0% bracket. This creates an opportunity to realize gains without federal tax on them, which can be useful for resetting cost basis or rebalancing a concentrated position. For example, someone between jobs, retired early before claiming benefits, or in a sabbatical year may have room in the 0% bracket. Always confirm the current thresholds, because a sale that pushes income higher can move part of the gain into a higher bracket.

Plan around income years: Gains realized in a low-income year may be taxed far less than the same gains realized in a high-income year. Coordinate sales with your expected income, and consider other income sources such as IRA withdrawals or bonuses.

The net investment income tax

Higher-income taxpayers may owe an additional 3.8% net investment income tax on investment income, including capital gains, once modified adjusted gross income exceeds certain thresholds. The thresholds are set by law and are not indexed in the same way as the capital gains brackets, so they affect more taxpayers over time. Include this surtax in your estimate if your income is high.

State taxes

Many states tax capital gains as ordinary income, and some have their own rules. A federal planning strategy can produce different results once state tax is included. Check your state's rules before making large sales.

A worked example

Suppose you bought shares for $5,000 and sold them for $9,000, a $4,000 gain. If you held them for eight months, the gain is short-term and taxed at your ordinary rate. If you held them for thirteen months, the gain is long-term and taxed at one of the preferential rates. Waiting a few weeks can therefore change the tax bill substantially. The numbers here are illustrative, and your actual tax depends on your full return.

Common mistakes

  • Selling a position just before it crosses the one-year mark without checking the difference in tax.
  • Forgetting that the 0%, 15%, and 20% thresholds depend on total taxable income.
  • Ignoring the net investment income tax at higher income levels.
  • Failing to track cost basis, which leads to paying tax on more gain than necessary.

Frequently asked questions

Are dividends taxed the same way?

Qualified dividends are generally taxed at the same preferential rates as long-term gains, subject to holding period rules. Non-qualified dividends are taxed as ordinary income.

Do capital losses offset gains?

Yes. Losses offset gains of the same type first, then gains of the other type. Net losses can offset limited ordinary income each year, as covered in our guide on tax-loss harvesting.

Does the holding period restart if I buy more shares?

Each lot of shares generally has its own purchase date and holding period. Track lots carefully, especially in taxable accounts with regular purchases.
