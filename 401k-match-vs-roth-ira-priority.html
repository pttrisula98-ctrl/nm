401(k) Match vs Roth IRA vs HSA: The Contribution Priority Order

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

When you have limited money to save for retirement, the order in which you fund accounts matters. A common approach is to capture any employer match first, then consider a health savings account, then a Roth or traditional IRA, and finally additional 401(k) savings. This is a useful default, not a fixed rule, and your income, tax bracket, and plan features can change the best sequence.

Step 1: Capture the full employer match

If your employer matches a portion of your 401(k) contributions, the match is effectively part of your pay. A match of 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to a percentage of salary, is an immediate return on that money before any investment growth. Contribute at least enough to receive the full match. Check your plan documents for the formula, because some matches require a vesting period, and some apply only to certain contribution types.

Step 2: Consider an HSA if you qualify

If you are enrolled in a high-deductible health plan, a health savings account offers a deduction on contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. Many people use it as a second retirement account after their medical spending needs are covered. Our guide to the HSA explains eligibility and the three tax advantages in more detail.

Step 3: Fund an IRA, Roth or traditional

Once the match and HSA are handled, many people turn to an IRA. A Roth IRA offers tax-free qualified withdrawals, while a traditional IRA may reduce taxes now. The choice depends on your current and expected tax rates and on whether your income is within the limits for direct Roth contributions. Our guide to Roth vs traditional IRAs walks through the framework. If your income is above the direct Roth limits, the backdoor Roth approach may be relevant, with its own rules.

Step 4: Increase 401(k) contributions

After the earlier steps, directing more money into your 401(k) increases your tax-advantaged savings, usually at a higher annual limit than IRAs allow. Workplace plans may offer a Roth 401(k) option, which combines the Roth tax treatment with the higher limit. Compare the investment options and fees in your plan, because high fees can reduce long-term growth.

This order is a common starting point, not a universal recommendation. Your situation may call for a different sequence.

When to break the order: You may want to put more into a 401(k) before an IRA if your IRA options are limited, or switch the order if your plan's fees are high. Some people prioritize paying down high-interest debt or building an emergency fund first.

Income limits and phase-outs

Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher income levels, and traditional IRA deductibility may be limited if you are covered by a workplace plan. These thresholds are set by the IRS and change periodically. If your income is near a limit, check the current figures and consider the timing of your contributions. The phase-outs are a major reason the priority order can shift from year to year.

Vesting, match formulas, and plan details

Before relying on a match, review how it vests. Some employers require several years of service before the match becomes fully yours, and leaving before vesting can forfeit part of it. Also check whether the match is paid per paycheck or once a year, since annual true-ups can affect how you time contributions.

Common mistakes

  • Not contributing enough to capture the full employer match.
  • Choosing an IRA over a 401(k) match without comparing the immediate return.
  • Contributing to an HSA without confirming HDHP eligibility.
  • Ignoring plan fees and investment choices inside the 401(k).
  • Assuming income limits are the same each year without checking.

Frequently asked questions

What if my employer does not offer a match?

The order changes. Many people then compare an IRA, HSA, and workplace plan based on tax treatment, fees, and investment options.

Should I always max out the HSA before the 401(k)?

Not necessarily. The right order depends on your match, your plan, and your cash flow. The priority list is a framework for comparison.

Can I contribute to both an IRA and a 401(k) in the same year?

Yes, if you meet the eligibility rules for each. Contribution limits apply separately to each type of account.
