CD Ladders: Capture Higher Rates Without Locking Up All Cash

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 8, 2026 · Updated November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

A certificate of deposit can pay a fixed rate for a set term, but it also ties up your money until maturity. A CD ladder solves that trade-off. You split your savings across several CDs with staggered maturity dates, so that a portion of your money becomes available at regular intervals while the rest continues to earn interest.

The strategy is simple to describe and easy to set up. It suits people who have cash they will not need for at least a year, and who want more predictability than a savings account offers. This guide explains how a ladder works, how to build one, and the situations where it may not be the right choice.

How a CD ladder works

Suppose you have $10,000 to save and want to spread it across five years. Instead of putting all of it into a single five-year CD, you divide it into five equal parts of $2,000. You buy one CD with each part, with maturities of one, two, three, four, and five years. When the one-year CD matures, you have $2,000 available. You can spend it, or you can reinvest it in a new five-year CD to keep the ladder going. Each year thereafter, another CD matures and the cycle repeats.

After the first cycle, every rung in the ladder is a five-year CD, and one matures each year. That gives you annual access to a portion of your money, while the average return reflects longer-term rates.

Why people use ladders

The main benefit is a balance between return and access. Longer CDs often pay higher fixed rates than shorter ones, but they lock up money. A ladder lets you earn some of that higher rate on part of your savings while keeping the rest liquid. It also reduces the risk of reinvesting all your money at a single point in time, because your rungs mature at different times and you reinvest at whatever rates are available then.

Ladders can also reduce the impact of early withdrawal penalties. If you need cash, you may only need to break one rung rather than your entire balance.

Choosing the length and spacing

Decide on the total length of the ladder based on how long you can comfortably leave the money invested. A three-year ladder with annual rungs gives you access every year and keeps the commitment short. A five-year ladder offers more time for longer-term rates but requires more patience. Some people use monthly or quarterly rungs for more frequent access, though that means managing more accounts.

Match the rungs to your needs: If you expect to need money every year, build rungs that mature annually. If your expenses are lumpy and less predictable, consider shorter rungs with more frequent maturities.

When a ladder beats a savings account

A CD ladder makes the most sense when you are confident you will not need the full balance soon, and when the fixed rates available on CDs are meaningfully higher than your savings account rate. It is less useful when rates are similar across terms, because you give up flexibility without a clear reward. It is also a poor fit for your emergency fund, which needs to be immediately accessible. Keep the emergency fund in a high-yield savings account, and use a ladder for money you are prepared to leave in place for longer.

Things to check before you open a ladder

Compare the early withdrawal penalties at each institution. Find out whether the CD renews automatically at maturity, and what rate it renews at, because some banks renew at a lower rate unless you act. Confirm that the institution is FDIC- or NCUA-insured, and keep balances within the insurance limits described in our guide on FDIC and NCUA insurance. Also check whether a CD has a minimum deposit or a callable feature, which allows the bank to end the CD early in some circumstances.

Common mistakes

  • Putting the emergency fund into CDs and then needing the money before maturity.
  • Forgetting that CDs renew automatically and missing the window to move the money.
  • Choosing terms based only on the highest advertised rate, without considering access.
  • Opening too many rungs at once, which becomes hard to track.

Frequently asked questions

Are CD ladders safe?

CDs at FDIC- or NCUA-insured institutions are protected up to the standard insurance limit. The main risk is that you cannot access the money without a penalty before maturity.

Do CD rates change?

Yes. Rates on new CDs change over time, which is one reason the ladder approach can help. Check current rates directly with the institution before deciding.

Are CD earnings taxable?

Generally, yes. Interest is taxable as ordinary income in the year it is earned, and you will usually receive a tax form for it. Consult a tax professional for details.
