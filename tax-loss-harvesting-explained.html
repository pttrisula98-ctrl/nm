Tax-Loss Harvesting: Turn Losses Into Tax Savings

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 20, 2026 · Updated November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment that has fallen below what you paid, so you can use the loss to offset taxable gains or, within limits, ordinary income. It applies only in taxable brokerage accounts, and it works best as a tool for managing a tax bill rather than a way to make money.

How the basic idea works

When you sell an investment for less than your cost basis, you realize a capital loss. Capital losses first offset capital gains of the same type, then gains of the other type, and any remaining net loss can offset up to a limited amount of ordinary income each year. Losses beyond those limits can generally be carried forward to future tax years. The exact rules, including limits and ordering, are set out by the IRS and should be checked against current guidance.

The strategy does not reduce the value of your portfolio on its own. It can lower your tax bill now, but if you sell and buy a similar investment, your market exposure stays roughly the same. The tax benefit comes from the loss itself, and the cost of waiting is that you have given up some potential future growth in the sold investment.

A simple example

Suppose you bought one fund for $10,000 and it is now worth $7,000, a $3,000 unrealized loss. You also have a $3,000 gain from selling another investment earlier in the year. If you sell the losing fund and realize the $3,000 loss, the loss can offset the gain, and your net taxable gain for the year becomes zero. You then reinvest the $7,000 in a different holding that keeps your exposure similar, but you must observe the wash sale rule described below.

The wash sale rule

The wash sale rule disallows a loss if you buy the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. The rule covers purchases in all your accounts, including retirement accounts and those of your spouse in many situations. If a wash sale applies, the loss is generally deferred and added to the cost basis of the new purchase rather than being lost entirely, but the timing benefit is delayed.

The phrase "substantially identical" is not precisely defined, which is why careful planning matters. Two different funds that track the same index may be treated differently from funds with meaningfully different holdings. Get guidance before relying on a particular replacement.

Watch the 61-day window: The wash sale period covers 30 days before and 30 days after the sale, plus the sale day itself. Automatic dividend reinvestment and recurring purchases can accidentally trigger it.

ETF pairings and replacements

Many investors replace a sold fund with a different fund that targets a similar market segment, for example a fund tracking a different index covering similar companies. This keeps the portfolio's allocation close to the target while avoiding a wash sale, provided the replacement is not substantially identical. The choice of replacement is a judgment call, and it carries tracking risk if the two funds perform differently while you hold them.

When it is worth the effort

Tax-loss harvesting is most useful when you have realized gains to offset, your income is high enough that tax savings are meaningful, or you have losses carried forward. It is less useful when your taxable account is small, your gains are minimal, or the trading costs and complexity outweigh the benefit. Some automated platforms handle harvesting for a fee, which should be weighed against the potential tax savings.

Be cautious about harvesting losses only to lower taxes in the current year if it leads to a higher tax bill later, for example by reducing your cost basis to the point where a future sale creates a larger gain. Consider the full picture over several years.

Common mistakes

  • Overlooking the wash sale rule when reinvesting in a similar fund.
  • Harvesting losses in a retirement account, where realized losses are not deductible.
  • Selling a fund and then letting automatic purchases trigger a wash sale.
  • Focusing only on the current year's tax rather than the overall plan.

Frequently asked questions

Can I harvest losses in an IRA?

Generally no. Losses inside traditional and Roth IRAs are not deductible, so harvesting is typically limited to taxable accounts.

Does tax-loss harvesting make my portfolio grow faster?

Not directly. It can reduce your taxes, which may leave more money invested, but the investment returns themselves depend on the holdings.

How much can I deduct against ordinary income?

The annual limit on net capital losses that offset ordinary income is set by tax law and can change. Check current IRS guidance for the amount that applies to you.
