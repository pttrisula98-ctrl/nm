Roth vs Traditional IRA: The Decision Framework

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

Both the Roth IRA and the traditional IRA are individual retirement accounts that offer tax advantages, but they apply those advantages at different points. A traditional IRA may reduce your taxes today, while a Roth IRA lets qualified withdrawals come out tax-free later. The right choice depends mostly on how your tax rate today compares with what you expect it to be in retirement.

This guide lays out the main factors, explains the rules that commonly apply, and gives you a checklist to revisit each year. It does not give a single answer, because the answer changes with your income, your plans, and tax law.

How each account is taxed

Traditional IRA. Contributions may be tax-deductible, depending on your income and whether you or your spouse have a workplace retirement plan. Investments grow tax-deferred. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

Roth IRA. Contributions are made with after-tax dollars and are not deductible. Investments grow tax-free. Qualified withdrawals, generally after age 59½ and after the account has been open for five years, are tax-free.

The core trade-off is simple. With a traditional IRA, you get the tax benefit now and pay tax later. With a Roth IRA, you pay tax now and withdraw tax-free later. If your tax rate is the same in both periods, the two accounts can produce similar after-tax results, all else being equal.

The core question: current versus future tax rate

Most of the decision comes down to comparing your marginal tax rate now with the rate you expect to pay when you withdraw. A worker in a relatively low bracket early in their career may find a Roth attractive, because today's tax is small. A high earner who expects a lower income in retirement may prefer a traditional IRA, because the deduction is worth more now than the taxes will cost later.

Expectations about future taxes are uncertain. Avoid building a plan on a single forecast. Many people split savings between account types so their retirement income has more than one tax treatment to draw from.

Income limits and eligibility

Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes, which means high earners may not be able to contribute directly. Traditional IRA deductibility also phases out if you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan and your income exceeds certain levels. The thresholds are adjusted periodically, so check the current figures from the IRS before making decisions. These limits are the reason some high earners use a strategy called a backdoor Roth, which we cover in our guide on the backdoor Roth IRA.

Required minimum distributions

Traditional IRAs require you to start taking minimum distributions after a certain age, generally in your 70s depending on your birth year. Those withdrawals are taxable and can push you into a higher bracket. Roth IRAs do not require withdrawals during the original owner's lifetime, which can make them useful for managing taxes in later years or passing assets to heirs. Our guide on required minimum distributions explains the timing and rules.

Conversions between the two

You can move money from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA through a conversion. The converted amount is generally added to your taxable income for the year, so the decision is a trade-off: you pay tax now to gain tax-free growth and withdrawals later. Conversions can make sense in low-income years, but they can also raise your tax bill, affect other benefits, and increase certain premiums. Model the effect before converting, and consider speaking with a tax professional.

Check each year: Your tax bracket, income, and plans change. A decision that fits this year may not fit next year, so reviewing the choice annually is part of the framework.

A yearly checklist

Use these questions each year. What is my current marginal tax rate, and what is it likely to be in retirement? Am I eligible to contribute directly to a Roth, or do I need another approach? Do I have a workplace plan that affects traditional IRA deductibility? How much flexibility do I want in retirement withdrawals? Do I have a plan for required distributions later? Would a conversion make sense in a lower-income year? Have any tax rules or limits changed?

Common mistakes

  • Choosing based on a single guess about future tax rates.
  • Ignoring income limits and contributing to a Roth when the contribution is not allowed.
  • Converting a large amount in one year without considering the tax bill.
  • Forgetting to make contributions by the tax-filing deadline for the year you intend to count them.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have both a Roth and a traditional IRA?

Yes. Annual contribution limits apply across all your IRAs combined, but you can hold both types of account.

What if I withdraw early?

Early withdrawals from either type can be subject to income tax and, in many cases, an additional penalty. Roth contributions can usually be withdrawn without tax or penalty, but earnings have different rules. Check the current IRS guidance.

Is a Roth always better?

No. A Roth is attractive in many situations, but a traditional account can be better when the current deduction is worth more than the future tax.
