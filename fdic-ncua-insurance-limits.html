FDIC and NCUA Insurance: Coverage Limits and Strategies

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 1, 2026 · Updated November 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Deposit insurance protects your money if a bank or credit union fails. The standard limit is $250,000 per depositor, but the way that limit applies is more specific than many people expect. Coverage depends on how the institution is insured, how many accounts you hold there, and how each account is titled.

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insures deposits at banks. The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) insures deposits at federal credit unions, and most state-chartered credit unions are insured through a comparable NCUA-backed program. This guide explains how coverage works, how common account types are treated, and how to check your own situation.

The basic rule

The standard insurance amount is $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, for each ownership category. Three parts of that sentence matter. "Per depositor" means that coverage belongs to a person, not to an account. "Per insured institution" means that the limit applies separately at each bank or credit union, so spreading money across institutions can increase total coverage. "Per ownership category" means that accounts held in different legal forms can each receive their own coverage.

Common ownership categories

The FDIC and NCUA recognize several categories, including single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts, and revocable trust accounts. Each category has its own limit, which means a single person can have coverage in more than one category at the same bank.

Account typeHow coverage generally works
Single accountsAll accounts owned by one person in their name alone are combined, up to $250,000 per institution.
Joint accountsAccounts owned by two or more people are insured separately from single accounts. Each co-owner is generally insured up to $250,000 for their share of joint deposits.
Certain retirement accountsTraditional and Roth IRAs held at one institution are generally insured up to $250,000 as a separate category.
Revocable trust accountsCoverage can increase based on the number of eligible beneficiaries, subject to the rules for trust accounts. Requirements must be met.

These are general descriptions. Specific rules include conditions, such as how beneficiaries are named and how trusts are documented. Check the FDIC or NCUA guidance for the details that apply to your accounts.

A worked example

Consider a person who has $200,000 in a single savings account and $150,000 in a checking account at the same bank, both in their own name. Those balances are combined into one single-ownership category, so $350,000 total exceeds the $250,000 limit, and the excess $100,000 is not insured at that bank. If they also hold a joint account at the same bank with a spouse, the joint balance may be insured separately, depending on the share each owner is considered to have. If they hold a traditional IRA at the same bank, that may be a separate category with its own limit.

The simplest way to protect the excess is to move part of it to a different insured institution, where it becomes a separate insured balance. That approach works only if the other institution is also FDIC- or NCUA-insured, so check each one.

Verify, don't assume: Use the FDIC's BankFind tool or the NCUA's credit union locator to confirm that an institution is insured before depositing money. Some apps and fintech platforms partner with banks, so confirm which bank holds your deposits.

Products that are not deposits

FDIC and NCUA insurance covers deposits, not investments. Money market funds, stocks, bonds, and mutual funds are not covered by deposit insurance, even if they are held at an insured bank. Treasury bills are backed by the U.S. government, but they are not deposits and are not covered by FDIC insurance. Keep this distinction in mind when you compare products.

Strategies for maximizing coverage

If your balances exceed the limits, you have several options. You can distribute funds across more than one insured institution. You can use different ownership categories, such as a joint account and an individual account, within the rules. You can use eligible trust accounts with properly documented beneficiaries. Or you can place part of the money in products that are not deposits, such as Treasury bills, with the trade-offs that come with them. Each approach has costs and requirements, so compare them before acting.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming that a bank's name or logo guarantees insurance without checking.
  • Believing a money market fund is insured the same way as a bank savings account.
  • Opening several accounts at one bank and assuming each one has its own full $250,000.
  • Forgetting that balances in accounts with the same owner and title are combined.

Frequently asked questions

Is the $250,000 limit per person or per account?

Per depositor, per institution, per ownership category. Multiple accounts in the same category at one institution share one limit.

What happens if a bank fails?

The FDIC or NCUA typically pays insured deposits, often quickly and by transferring accounts to another institution. Uninsured amounts may be recovered through the failed institution's assets, but recovery is not guaranteed. Check the agency's website for current procedures.

Do I need to do anything to get coverage?

Coverage is automatic at insured institutions, but the correct account titling and beneficiary designations must be in place for the categories you rely on.
