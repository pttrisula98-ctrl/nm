Buying T-Bills on TreasuryDirect: Step-by-Step

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 4, 2026 · Updated November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Treasury bills are short-term debt issued by the U.S. Treasury, and TreasuryDirect is the government's website for buying them directly, without a brokerage. The process takes some patience the first time, but once it is set up, buying and reinvesting bills is straightforward.

This guide walks through the main steps: opening an account, understanding how auctions work, placing a bid, choosing what happens at maturity, and handling the tax paperwork. It assumes you already know what T-bills are. If you are comparing them with other cash options, start with our overview of high-yield savings, money market accounts, CDs, and T-bills.

Step 1: Open a TreasuryDirect account

Go to the official TreasuryDirect website and create an individual account. You will need your Social Security number, a U.S. address, a bank account for transfers, and identity verification. The application can take several days to be approved, so start early if you have a specific auction date in mind. Set up two-factor authentication and keep your login details secure, because the account holds government securities in your name.

Link a bank account in your name. Purchases are paid by debit from that account, and any maturing money can be returned to it.

Step 2: Understand how T-bills are sold

T-bills are sold at regular auctions. Each bill is sold at a discount to its face value, which is the amount you receive at maturity. The difference between your purchase price and the face value is your interest. Common terms include four-week, eight-week, thirteen-week, twenty-six-week, and fifty-two-week bills. Auction results and upcoming schedules are published on the TreasuryDirect website and the official Treasury site.

You do not earn interest in regular periodic payments. Instead, you buy at a discount and receive the full face value when the bill matures. This is why the price you pay depends on the rate set at auction.

Step 3: Choose a bid type

There are two ways to bid. A non-competitive bid accepts whatever rate is set at auction. You receive the bills you request at that rate, up to a set maximum amount per auction. This is the simplest option and suits most individual investors. A competitive bid specifies the rate you are willing to accept. If your rate is too high, you may receive no bills, and you risk getting a lower rate than you hoped for. Most individuals use non-competitive bids because they avoid this uncertainty.

Bids generally must be placed before the auction closes, and the purchase amount is typically specified in increments of $100 with a minimum of $100. Check the current auction announcement for the exact limits before you bid.

Non-competitiveThe amount of bills you wantYou accept the auction rate, which keeps things simple

Step 4: Set up reinvestment

When a bill matures, TreasuryDirect can either pay the proceeds to your linked bank account or reinvest them automatically in a new bill of the same term. Reinvestment is useful if you want to keep the money working without buying manually each time. You can change these settings later, but make sure the choice reflects your plan, because a bill that reinvests will keep your money locked up for another term.

Plan the ladder: If you want regular access to cash, stagger your purchases across different maturities, similar to a CD ladder. This way, a portion of your money matures at predictable times.

Step 5: Understand the tax treatment

Interest on T-bills is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from state and local income tax. This exemption can make T-bills more attractive if you live in a state with high income taxes. You generally report the interest in the year the bill matures, and TreasuryDirect provides the tax information you need. Keep your annual statements, and consult a tax professional about how the rules apply to your situation.

Step 6: Track your bills

Log in regularly to check your holdings, maturity dates, and reinvestment settings. Set calendar reminders a few days before a bill matures so you can decide whether to reinvest, spend, or move the money. If you ever need to sell a bill before maturity, that is generally done through a brokerage or a secondary market rather than TreasuryDirect, and the sale price may be higher or lower than the amount you paid.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that reinvestment is on by default for some bills, so money stays locked when you wanted cash.
  • Placing a competitive bid at a rate too high or too low to be accepted.
  • Assuming T-bill interest is exempt from federal tax. It is not.
  • Using a third-party website instead of the official TreasuryDirect site.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying through TreasuryDirect safe?

The securities are backed by the U.S. government. The main safety step is protecting your own account, with a strong password and two-factor authentication. Only use the official TreasuryDirect website.

Can I sell a T-bill early?

TreasuryDirect is designed mainly for holding bills to maturity. Selling before maturity typically happens through a brokerage or another market, where the price can fluctuate.

What is the minimum amount I can invest?

Minimums and increments are set for each auction and can change. Check the current auction announcement on TreasuryDirect or the official Treasury site before bidding.
