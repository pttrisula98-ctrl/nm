Credit Score Factors: What Actually Moves the Needle

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 17, 2026 · Updated November 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Your credit score is a number that summarizes how reliably you have repaid borrowed money. Lenders, landlords, and some insurers use it to judge risk. The score is built from several factors, and knowing their relative weight helps you focus on the habits that matter most.

The most widely used consumer scores in the United States are FICO and VantageScore. Their exact formulas are proprietary, and the versions differ, but they draw on the same core categories of information from your credit reports. The weights below are approximate and drawn from the public descriptions of the most common FICO model.

The five main factors

FactorApproximate weightWhat it measures
Payment historyAbout 35%Whether you pay on time, including late payments and collections
Amounts owedAbout 30%Balances relative to credit limits, often called utilization
Length of credit historyAbout 15%The age of your oldest and average accounts
New creditAbout 10%Recent applications and newly opened accounts
Credit mixAbout 10%The variety of account types, such as cards, installment loans, and mortgages

Payment history: the largest factor

Paying on time is the single most important habit. A payment reported as 30 or more days late can lower your score substantially, and the impact is larger for people whose scores were previously high. Late payments usually remain on your report for several years, although their effect fades over time. Setting up automatic minimum payments is a simple safeguard, and you can always pay more manually.

Collections, charge-offs, and public records also weigh heavily. If you have one of these, the most useful step is to bring the account current or resolve it, and then keep every future payment on time.

Amounts owed and utilization

Credit utilization is the share of your available revolving credit that you are using. Lower is generally better, and a common guideline is to keep utilization well below 30% of your limits, with lower levels often associated with stronger scores. Utilization is calculated across individual cards and across all cards combined, so one maxed-out card can hurt even if your total usage looks reasonable. Our guide on credit utilization explains how statement dates affect what gets reported.

Utilization moves fast: Unlike payment history, utilization has no memory. Once balances fall, the effect on your score can appear after the next reporting cycle.

Length of credit history

Scores reward a longer track record. Keeping old accounts open, even if you use them rarely, helps your average account age. Closing a long-standing card can shorten your average age and reduce your available credit, which may raise utilization. If a card has no annual fee and you no longer want it, consider keeping it open and using it occasionally for a small purchase.

New credit and hard inquiries

When you apply for credit, the lender typically makes a hard inquiry, which can lower your score slightly and for a limited time. Opening several new accounts in a short period can also reduce your average account age. Rate shopping for certain loans, such as mortgages or auto loans, is usually treated as a single inquiry if done within a short window, but the exact rules vary by scoring model.

Credit mix

Lenders like to see that you can handle different types of credit responsibly, such as revolving cards and installment loans. This factor carries less weight than the others, and you should not open accounts you do not need just to improve your mix. Responsible management of the accounts you already have matters more.

Practical levers, in order of impact

For most people, the most effective steps are these: pay every bill on time, reduce revolving balances, avoid unnecessary applications, keep older accounts open when sensible, and check your credit reports for errors. Disputing inaccurate information can improve your report, and you can request free reports from the three national bureaus through the official government site.

Common mistakes

  • Believing that carrying a balance improves your score. Paying in full is generally the better habit.
  • Closing an old card with no annual fee and no good reason, which can raise utilization and shorten history.
  • Applying for several cards at once because you think you need a higher limit.
  • Ignoring errors on a credit report, which can drag down a score unfairly.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly can my score change?

Changes can appear after the next reporting cycle, often within a month. Negative items like late payments can stay on your report for years, but their effect usually weakens over time.

Do I need to carry a balance to build credit?

No. Paying your statement in full keeps you out of interest charges and still reports activity. Carrying a balance is not required.

Why do my scores differ between apps?

Different scoring models, different credit bureaus, and different reporting dates can all produce different numbers. Focus on the trend rather than a single figure.
