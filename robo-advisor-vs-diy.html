Robo-Advisor vs DIY: Fees, Features, and Who Should Use Which

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published October 28, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026 · 8 min read

A robo-advisor is an automated service that builds and maintains a diversified portfolio for you, usually for an annual fee charged as a percentage of assets. A do-it-yourself (DIY) approach means you choose your own funds, set your allocation, and rebalance on your own schedule. Both can work well, and the right choice depends on how much you value convenience compared with cost.

What a robo-advisor does

After a short questionnaire about your goals, time horizon, and tolerance for risk, a robo-advisor assigns you a target allocation, usually built from low-cost index funds or ETFs. It invests your money, reinvests dividends, rebalances automatically when allocations drift, and in some cases harvests tax losses in taxable accounts. Many services also offer automatic deposits, goal tracking, and access to human advisors for an additional cost.

What DIY investing involves

With DIY investing, you open an account at a brokerage, pick the funds or ETFs you want, decide your allocation, and handle rebalancing and contributions yourself. A simple DIY portfolio, such as one built from a few broad index funds, can be very low cost. It also requires you to stay disciplined, keep records, and review the portfolio regularly.

Comparing the costs

Robo-advisors typically charge an annual advisory fee as a percentage of the assets they manage, in addition to the expense ratios of the funds they hold. DIY investors pay only the expense ratios of the funds they choose, plus any trading costs their brokerage charges. The advisory fee is the main difference, and it compounds over time, which is why costs matter so much for long-term investors.

Break-even math

Suppose a robo-advisor charges an advisory fee that adds a fixed percentage per year on top of the fund costs you would pay anyway. Over a 30-year horizon, even a small annual percentage can reduce the final balance noticeably, because the fee is charged each year on a growing balance. Ask yourself whether the services the robo-advisor provides, such as automatic rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting, are worth more to you than the annual cost. For some investors, avoiding mistakes and staying invested is worth the fee. For others, doing it themselves is simple and cheap enough that the fee is hard to justify.

Ask the right question: Not "Is the fee high?" but "Will I actually do the things a DIY portfolio requires, every year, without fail?" Your honest answer decides more than the fee alone.

Who might prefer a robo-advisor

A robo-advisor may suit you if you want a hands-off approach, tend to delay or avoid financial tasks, are prone to emotional trading, or have a complex situation that benefits from automated tax management. It can also be useful for a first investment because the setup guides you through allocation decisions.

Who might prefer DIY

DIY may suit you if you are comfortable with a simple portfolio, enjoy learning about investing, want to minimize ongoing costs, and will rebalance on a regular schedule. A three-fund style portfolio is often easy to run this way.

Other options to consider

The choice is not always binary. Some brokerages offer automated portfolios at low or no advisory fees, and some investors combine approaches, using a robo-advisor for one account and managing another themselves. Hybrid options can give you automation where it helps most and cost control elsewhere.

Common mistakes

  • Paying an advisory fee without understanding what it covers.
  • Choosing DIY for cost reasons and then failing to rebalance or contribute consistently.
  • Assuming a robo-advisor's allocation matches your personal risk tolerance without reviewing it.
  • Comparing only the headline fee and ignoring fund expense ratios and account minimums.

Frequently asked questions

Are robo-advisors safe?

Verify that the firm is a registered investment adviser or broker-dealer, check its disclosures, and confirm that your assets are held in your name at a qualified custodian. Investments can still lose value.

Can I switch later?

Yes, but moving between a robo-advisor and a DIY account can trigger taxable sales in taxable accounts. Plan the transition carefully.

Do I need a lot of money to start?

Requirements vary. Some services have low or no minimums, while others require a certain balance. Check the terms before opening an account.
