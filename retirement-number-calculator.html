Calculate Your Retirement Number: The 4% Rule and Beyond

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 16, 2026 · Updated November 16, 2026 · 9 min read

Your retirement number is the amount of savings that could support your planned spending for the rest of your life. Most estimates start with a simple formula: annual spending divided by a withdrawal rate. The formula is easy to compute, but the inputs and assumptions decide whether the answer is realistic.

Start with spending, not salary

Estimate the annual spending you expect in retirement, not the income you earn now. Some expenses fall after work ends, such as commuting and some work clothing, while others rise, such as healthcare and travel. Begin with your current essential and discretionary spending from bank statements, then adjust for changes you expect. Subtract income you know will continue, such as Social Security or a pension, to find the amount your savings must cover.

For example, suppose you expect to spend $60,000 a year in retirement and will receive $24,000 a year from Social Security. Your savings would need to cover the remaining $36,000.

The 4% rule

The 4% rule is a widely cited guideline from research on historical US market returns. It suggests that withdrawing about 4% of a portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjusting that amount for inflation each year, had a high historical success rate over long periods. Dividing the savings needed by 4% gives a rough target. In the example above, $36,000 divided by 0.04 is $900,000.

The limits of the rule

The 4% rule has important limits. It was derived from historical data, and future returns, inflation, and interest rates may differ. Retirements can last longer than the period studied, and the rule does not account for taxes, fees, or large one-time expenses. Retirees who begin withdrawals during a market downturn face a particular risk, discussed below.

Sequence of returns risk

Sequence of returns risk is the danger that poor market returns early in retirement reduce a portfolio more than the same returns later would. When you are withdrawing money, losses early on force you to sell more shares at low prices, which leaves less to recover when markets rise. Two retirees with the same average return can end up in very different positions depending on the order of returns. Holding a cash reserve or a bond allocation for near-term spending can reduce the need to sell during a downturn.

Build a buffer: Many retirees keep one to two years of planned withdrawals in cash or short-term bonds. This can allow them to avoid selling stocks during a decline.

Flexible withdrawal strategies

Rather than withdrawing a fixed inflation-adjusted amount, some retirees adjust spending when markets fall and allow increases when markets rise. Others use a percentage of the current portfolio each year, which varies with market value. Flexible approaches can improve long-term sustainability, but they require a willingness to change spending. Combining a modest guaranteed income floor with flexible spending on discretionary items is a common compromise.

Monte Carlo reality checks

Monte Carlo simulations run thousands of random return scenarios to estimate the share in which a withdrawal plan lasts. They can reveal risks that a single average return hides. The results depend on assumptions about returns, volatility, inflation, and fees, so treat them as a range rather than a guarantee. Many online calculators use this approach, and it is worth testing your plan under several assumptions.

Common mistakes

  • Using current salary as the spending target rather than expected retirement spending.
  • Ignoring healthcare costs before Medicare eligibility and after it.
  • Forgetting taxes on withdrawals from traditional accounts.
  • Assuming a single withdrawal rate fits every retirement timeline.
  • Not accounting for the possibility of a long retirement.

Frequently asked questions

Is 4% always safe?

No withdrawal rate is guaranteed. The 4% figure is a historical guideline that may be too high or too low for your situation.

How does Social Security change the number?

Guaranteed income reduces the amount your savings must cover. The timing of Social Security claims can significantly affect benefits, which is covered in our guide on when to claim Social Security.

Should I include my home?

A home can provide housing and, in some cases, funds through a sale or a reverse mortgage. Treat home equity separately from investable savings and understand the costs of any option.
