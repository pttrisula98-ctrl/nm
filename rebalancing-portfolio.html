Portfolio Rebalancing: When, How, and Why

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published October 31, 2026 · Updated October 31, 2026 · 7 min read

Rebalancing brings your portfolio back to its target mix after markets have moved the weights. It is one of the few investment tasks that is both simple and effective, and it helps keep your risk level consistent with the plan you chose. The main challenges are deciding how often to act and managing the tax cost of selling.

Why portfolios drift

Suppose you start with a 60% stock and 40% bond portfolio. If stocks rise strongly for two years while bonds stay flat, the mix might shift to 70% stocks and 30% bonds. Your portfolio is now riskier than you intended, even though you did nothing. Rebalancing reverses that drift by selling some of the asset that has grown and buying more of the asset that has lagged.

Rebalancing is also a discipline. It forces you to trim what has recently done well and add to what has recently done poorly, which runs against the emotional pull of chasing returns.

Two main methods

Calendar rebalancing checks the portfolio on a fixed schedule, such as once a year or twice a year, and restores the target weights at each check. It is simple to follow and easy to plan around. Its drawback is that a large drift between checks can go unaddressed for months.

Threshold rebalancing acts only when an asset class moves beyond a set band around its target, such as five percentage points. It responds to market moves when they matter, and it can avoid unnecessary trading during calm periods. It requires more monitoring, and it can trigger trades at inconvenient times.

Many investors use a combined approach, checking once or twice a year and acting only if an allocation has drifted beyond a set band. Either pure method is acceptable. Choose one, write it down, and follow it.

The tax question

Rebalancing in a taxable brokerage account can trigger capital gains taxes. When you sell an investment that has risen in value, the gain is generally taxable in the year of the sale, subject to the rules that apply to your holding period and income. Selling a position at a loss can produce a deductible capital loss, but the rules for those losses have their own limits and conditions.

In tax-advantaged accounts such as traditional and Roth IRAs or 401(k) plans, selling within the account generally does not create a current tax bill, which makes rebalancing there simpler.

Rebalancing without selling

There are several ways to move toward your target without selling appreciated holdings in a taxable account:

Direct new contributions. Invest new money into the underweight asset until the mix returns to target. This works best when you are still adding money regularly.

Use dividends and interest. Redirect income payments from the overweight asset into the underweight one.

Rebalance in retirement accounts. If you hold the same funds in a tax-advantaged account, adjusting there may achieve the target with less tax friction.

Check the whole portfolio: Measure allocation across all accounts, not one at a time. A bond fund in an IRA and a stock fund in a taxable account count toward the same overall mix.

A worked example

Consider a portfolio of $100,000 with a target of 60% stocks and 40% bonds. After a strong year for stocks, the holdings are $68,000 in stocks and $32,000 in bonds, which is 68% and 32%. To restore the target, the investor needs $60,000 in stocks and $40,000 in bonds. They could sell $8,000 of stocks and buy $8,000 of bonds, or direct the next $8,000 of contributions into bonds until the split is restored. The second route avoids the sale but takes longer. Both achieve the same target allocation.

Common mistakes

  • Rebalancing too often, which increases costs and taxes without clear benefit.
  • Never rebalancing, which lets risk drift well beyond your plan.
  • Ignoring the tax consequences of selling in a taxable account.
  • Abandoning the rebalancing plan during a sharp market move, which is often when it matters most.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I rebalance?

Once a year is a common and sensible schedule for many investors. Threshold-based rules can be used alongside it.

Does rebalancing improve returns?

Its main purpose is to control risk and keep the portfolio aligned with your plan. It may or may not improve returns in a given period.

Should I rebalance after a market crash?

If your plan calls for it, rebalancing can mean buying the asset that has fallen. Make sure this fits your risk tolerance and time horizon before acting.
