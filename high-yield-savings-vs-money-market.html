High-Yield Savings vs Money Market vs CDs vs T-Bills

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Several safe places to hold cash now pay meaningful interest, and the names are confusing: high-yield savings accounts, money market accounts, money market funds, certificates of deposit, and Treasury bills. They look similar on the surface, but they differ in how fast you can access your money, how your principal is protected, and how the interest is taxed.

This guide compares the four options on the features that matter for short-term money: yield, liquidity, safety, minimums, and taxes. It ends with a decision guide to help you match each option to a specific goal. Rates change frequently, so the examples below describe how each product works rather than quoting current numbers.

The short answer

For money you may need within a few months, such as an emergency fund, a high-yield savings account is usually the simplest choice. For money you know you will not touch for a fixed period, a CD or a Treasury bill can offer a predictable return. A money market fund is often used by people who want a brokerage-based cash position. A bank money market account sits between a savings account and a money market fund, with check-writing or debit access in some cases.

High-yield savings accounts

A high-yield savings account is a deposit account at a bank or credit union that pays a higher interest rate than a traditional savings account. Most allow unlimited deposits and withdrawals, though some banks still limit certain transfers. Balances are typically insured by the FDIC (banks) or NCUA (credit unions) up to the standard limit of $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category.

The rate is variable. The bank can raise or lower it at any time, which means the yield you see today may not last. Online-only banks often pay more than branch-based banks because their overhead is lower, but the principle is the same: check that the institution is FDIC- or NCUA-insured before you open an account.

Money market accounts and money market funds

These two products have similar names but are different things, and the difference matters.

A bank money market account (MMA) is a deposit account. Like a savings account, it is insured by the FDIC or NCUA up to the standard limit. Some offer limited check-writing or a debit card. Rates are variable and often close to high-yield savings rates.

A money market fund (MMF) is a type of mutual fund offered through a brokerage or investment firm. It invests in short-term, high-quality debt such as government securities and commercial paper. It is regulated by the SEC, not insured by the FDIC, and it is designed to keep a stable share price, typically one dollar per share. Losses are rare but not impossible, which is why these funds are not the same as an insured bank deposit.

Key distinction: A bank money market account is FDIC or NCUA insured. A money market fund is not insured by those agencies, even though it is a very low-risk investment. Read the product documents to confirm which one you are buying.

Certificates of deposit (CDs)

A CD is a time deposit. You agree to leave your money with a bank for a set term, such as three months, one year, or five years, in exchange for a fixed rate. The rate is locked for the term, which gives you predictability. CDs are FDIC or NCUA insured within the same limits.

The trade-off is liquidity. If you withdraw before the maturity date, you usually pay an early withdrawal penalty, often a set number of months of interest. Some banks offer "no-penalty" CDs with more flexibility but often a lower rate. A CD ladder, where you spread money across several maturity dates, can balance return and access. Our guide on CD ladders explains how that works.

Treasury bills (T-bills)

T-bills are short-term debt issued by the U.S. Treasury, with maturities of a few days up to 52 weeks. You buy them at a discount to face value and receive the full face value at maturity. The difference is your interest. T-bills are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government and are not subject to FDIC limits, because they are not deposits.

Interest from T-bills is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from state and local income tax. For someone living in a high-tax state, that exemption can make a noticeable difference. You can buy T-bills directly through TreasuryDirect or through a brokerage account. T-bills can be sold before maturity, but the price may be higher or lower than what you paid, so they carry a small amount of market risk if you need to sell early.

A decision guide by goal

The right product depends less on which pays the most this week and more on when you will need the money. If the money is your emergency fund, prioritize access and insurance over a slightly higher rate, which points toward a high-yield savings account. If you are saving for a purchase in 9 to 12 months, a CD or T-bill with a matching maturity date can lock in a rate and remove the temptation to spend. If you live in a state with high income tax and have a large cash balance, a T-bill may leave you with more after-tax interest than a taxable account paying a similar rate. If you already hold a brokerage account, a money market fund can be a convenient place for uninvested cash, but remember it is not insured.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a money market fund is FDIC insured.
  • Chasing the highest advertised rate without checking whether it is a promotional rate that will change.
  • Holding more than the insurance limit at one bank without knowing how to split it across institutions or ownership categories.
  • Locking all cash into a long CD and then needing the money early.
  • Forgetting that interest from all these products is generally taxable as ordinary income in the year it is earned, and that you will receive a tax form for it.

Frequently asked questions

Is a high-yield savings account safe?

If the bank or credit union is FDIC or NCUA insured, deposits are protected up to the standard limit. Verify insurance on the institution's website or with the regulator's bank or credit union lookup tools.

Which is better for an emergency fund?

Most people prefer a high-yield savings account or a bank money market account because the money is accessible quickly and insured. Splitting the fund between two products can also work well.

Do I need to pay taxes on this interest?

Yes, interest from these products is generally taxable as ordinary income in the year you receive it, with the exception that T-bill interest is exempt from state and local income tax. Consult a tax professional for your specific situation.
