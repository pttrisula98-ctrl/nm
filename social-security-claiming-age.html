When to Claim Social Security: Break-Even Analysis

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 3, 2026 · Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read

You can start Social Security retirement benefits as early as age 62, but the amount you receive depends on when you claim. Claiming early means a smaller monthly check for life. Waiting past full retirement age increases it. The right decision depends on your health, other income, marital situation, and how long you expect to live.

How claiming age changes your benefit

Your Social Security benefit is based on your lifetime earnings and is calculated as a "primary insurance amount" at your full retirement age (FRA). Full retirement age is 67 for people born in 1960 or later, and it is earlier for people born before that. Claiming before FRA reduces the monthly benefit permanently. Claiming after FRA earns delayed retirement credits, which increase the benefit for each year you wait, up to age 70. After 70, waiting produces no further increase.

Claiming ageEffect on benefit (FRA 67)
62Reduced by roughly 30%
67 (full retirement age)100% of the calculated benefit
70Increased by roughly 24%, from delayed credits

These percentages reflect the standard rules for someone with a full retirement age of 67. The exact reduction or increase depends on your birth year and claiming month, so check your own figures on the official Social Security website.

The break-even idea

Claiming early gives you checks sooner, while waiting gives you larger checks later. The break-even age is the point at which the cumulative benefits from waiting catch up with the cumulative benefits from claiming earlier. Break-even calculations are simple to set up, but they are sensitive to assumptions, including how long you live and whether you would invest early benefits.

For example, if claiming at 62 gives you a benefit of $1,800 per month and waiting until 70 gives you about $2,200 per month, the larger check at 70 may overtake the total early benefits somewhere in the early-to-mid 80s, depending on how you compare the two. Under this simple comparison, a longer life tends to favor waiting, while a shorter life tends to favor claiming early. Use your own estimated benefit from the official statement, not these illustrative figures.

Check your statement: Your personal Social Security statement shows estimated benefits at different claiming ages. Review it before making a decision.

Other factors to weigh

Health and family history. If you have significant health concerns, claiming earlier may make sense. If you expect a long life, waiting may be more valuable.

Other income and savings. If you can cover living expenses from savings while delaying, waiting can provide a larger lifetime guarantee. If you must draw on savings that would otherwise last, claiming earlier may be necessary.

Working while claiming. If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, earnings above an annual limit can temporarily reduce benefits. The limit changes each year, and the withheld amount is generally recalculated at full retirement age.

Taxes. Depending on your total income, a portion of Social Security benefits may be taxable. Plan for the tax effect of benefits along with other withdrawals.

Spousal and survivor benefits

Married people may be eligible for spousal benefits based on a partner's earnings record, up to a set share of the partner's benefit at full retirement age, subject to rules. Survivor benefits can provide a surviving spouse with the higher of the two benefits. Because a larger benefit can continue as a survivor benefit, the higher earner's claiming decision often affects the couple's income for life. Coordinating claims within a couple is a common reason to delay the larger benefit.

The file-and-suspend myth

File-and-suspend was a strategy that let a person file for benefits to allow a spouse to claim a spousal benefit while the person's own benefit continued to grow. Federal law changed this option, and for most people it is no longer available in the way it once was. Do not rely on older articles describing it. Confirm current rules with the Social Security Administration before attempting any unusual claiming strategy.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming at 62 without comparing the lifetime difference.
  • Ignoring the survivor benefit effect for a married couple.
  • Forgetting the earnings test when working before full retirement age.
  • Relying on outdated strategies such as file-and-suspend.
  • Using break-even figures without checking your own estimated benefit.

Frequently asked questions

Can I change my mind after claiming?

There is a limited window to withdraw an application, subject to rules and repayment of benefits received. Check the current SSA rules if this matters to you.

Does claiming later mean I get more over my lifetime?

Not always. It depends on how long you live, your health, and your household's needs. The break-even comparison helps frame the decision.

Where do I start?

Create or log in to your account at SSA.gov to review your statement and estimated benefits.
