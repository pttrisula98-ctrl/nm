Required Minimum Distributions: Rules, Penalties, and Strategies

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 7, 2026 · Updated November 7, 2026 · 7 min read

Traditional IRAs and most workplace retirement plans eventually require you to withdraw a minimum amount each year, whether you need the money or not. These required minimum distributions, or RMDs, are taxed as ordinary income and can raise your tax bill. Knowing when they start, how they are calculated, and how to plan around them can make a meaningful difference in retirement.

When RMDs begin

Under current federal law, the age at which RMDs begin depends on your birth year. Generally, the starting age is 73 for people born in the early 1950s through the late 1950s, and 75 for people born in 1960 or later. Your first RMD can be delayed until April 1 of the year after you reach the starting age, but doing so means two distributions in one tax year, which can increase your taxes. Confirm the starting age that applies to you, because the rules have changed several times in recent years.

How the amount is calculated

Each year's RMD is generally your account balance as of the end of the prior year, divided by a life expectancy factor published in IRS tables. The factor decreases as you age, so the required percentage increases over time. For example, a balance of $500,000 divided by a factor of 25 produces a required distribution of $20,000. The IRS publishes the tables, and the factor you use depends on the table that applies to your situation.

Penalties for missed withdrawals

Failing to take a required distribution has historically triggered a significant excise tax on the amount not withdrawn. Recent law reduced this penalty in many cases, and it may be reduced further if the shortfall is corrected in a timely way. Because the rules and correction processes are specific, the safest approach is to take each RMD on schedule and, if you miss one, to correct it promptly and document the steps you took.

Set a reminder: Calendar each year's RMD deadline. Most custodians offer automatic withdrawal options that can take the required amount on a schedule you choose.

Qualified charitable distributions

Once you reach the age at which RMDs apply, you may be able to send up to a federally set annual limit directly from an IRA to a qualified charity. A qualified charitable distribution, or QCD, counts toward your RMD but is excluded from taxable income. This can be useful if you give to charity and do not itemize deductions. QCD rules have specific conditions, such as the type of recipient and how the transfer is made, so confirm them before acting.

Managing the tax impact

Strategies to reduce the tax effect of RMDs include converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth in lower-income years before RMDs begin, using QCDs for charitable giving, and coordinating withdrawals with other income. Spreading withdrawals across years may also help if your income is near a bracket threshold. Each strategy has trade-offs, and the right mix depends on your full financial picture. Inherited accounts

Heirs who inherit traditional IRAs or workplace plan accounts face their own distribution rules. Many non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the inherited account within a set number of years after the owner's death, and some must take annual distributions during that period. Spouse beneficiaries have additional options. The rules depend on the relationship to the owner, the owner's age at death, and whether the owner had started RMDs. Confirm the rules that apply before taking distributions.

Roth accounts

Roth IRAs do not require distributions during the original owner's lifetime. Roth accounts in workplace plans were subject to RMD rules in the past, but current law has changed that for many plans. Check your plan's rules if you hold a Roth 401(k).

Common mistakes

  • Taking the first RMD in the same year as the second, which can push income into a higher bracket.
  • Miscalculating the amount by using the wrong balance or table.
  • Missing an RMD for an account you forgot about, such as an old 401(k).
  • Forgetting that RMDs from multiple IRAs may be combined, but RMDs from workplace plans must be taken separately.
  • Assuming the rules for inherited accounts match those for your own accounts.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take more than the required amount?

Yes. Extra withdrawals are taxable but do not reduce future RMDs in the same way. Consider your tax situation before withdrawing more than required.

Do RMDs apply if I still work?

For traditional IRAs, RMDs generally apply regardless of work status. Some workplace plans offer exceptions for current employees who do not own a significant stake in the employer, so check the plan rules.

Can I roll an RMD into another account?

Generally, RMDs cannot be rolled over into another retirement account. They are taxable distributions.
