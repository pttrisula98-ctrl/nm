Backdoor Roth IRA: Step-by-Step and Pro-Rata Trap

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 13, 2026 · Updated November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

A backdoor Roth IRA is a two-step method some higher earners use to move money into a Roth IRA when their income is too high to contribute to one directly. It is legal under current law, but it involves paperwork, and one rule, the pro-rata rule, can make a conversion more expensive than it looks.

This guide explains the mechanics, shows the reporting requirements, and walks through the pro-rata trap. Because the rules and income thresholds change, verify the current figures with the IRS before acting.

Why the backdoor exists

Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher income levels, so above a certain threshold you cannot contribute directly. Traditional IRA contributions are not subject to the same income limit for making them, but the deduction may be limited if you or your spouse are covered by a workplace retirement plan. The backdoor approach uses that gap: you make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution, then convert it to a Roth.

The two basic steps

Step one: Contribute to a traditional IRA. Because your income is above the deduction limit for this purpose, you make the contribution as nondeductible, which means you have already paid tax on that money. Your basis, the portion that has already been taxed, equals the amount you contributed.

Step two: Convert the traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. In a perfect case, the conversion adds little or no taxable income, because you already paid tax on the contribution and it has not grown much in the meantime. Any growth between the contribution and the conversion is taxable.

Speed matters: Converting soon after the contribution keeps taxable growth low. Some people convert within days or weeks, but the right timing depends on your situation.

Reporting with Form 8606

You report nondeductible traditional IRA contributions and conversions on IRS Form 8606, which is filed with your federal tax return. Form 8606 tracks your basis in traditional IRAs, and it is essential for showing that the converted amount was already taxed. Without accurate records, you may end up paying tax twice on the same money. Keep copies of all forms and account statements for each year.

The pro-rata trap

The pro-rata rule is where many backdoor conversions become more expensive than expected. When you convert, the IRS generally treats all of your traditional IRAs as one combined pool for tax purposes, including pre-tax balances from past deductible contributions, rollovers from workplace plans, and any earnings. Your converted amount is taxed in proportion to the share of the pool that was pre-tax.

For example, suppose you contribute $7,000 nondeductibly, but you also hold $93,000 in a traditional IRA that was funded with pre-tax dollars. Your total traditional IRA balance is $100,000, and only $7,000 of it is after-tax. If you convert the $7,000, only 7% of the conversion is tax-free. The other 93% is taxable, even though you only converted the new contribution.

Tax-free share of a $7,000 conversion7%, or about $490
Taxable share of a $7,000 conversion93%, or about $6,510

These figures are illustrative. The pro-rata calculation also considers the value of your accounts at year-end, which can differ from the amount you contributed.

Ways people deal with the pro-rata rule

Some people address the pro-rata issue before converting. One common approach is to move pre-tax traditional IRA balances into a workplace retirement plan, if that plan accepts incoming rollovers. This can reduce the pre-tax balance counted in the pro-rata calculation. The rules for such transfers are specific, so check the plan's terms and get guidance before attempting it. Another approach is to accept the taxable portion and consider whether the conversion still makes sense for your situation.

Is it worth it?

The backdoor Roth can be valuable for people who expect substantial tax-free growth and want Roth flexibility. Its value falls if you have large pre-tax IRA balances, because the pro-rata rule will make the conversion largely taxable. Also consider the paperwork, the possibility that rules change, and whether a workplace plan offers better options. Compare it with other tax-advantaged choices, including a Roth 401(k) or HSA, where available.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring existing pre-tax IRA balances when calculating the conversion tax.
  • Failing to file Form 8606, which can lead to paying tax twice on the same money.
  • Investing the nondeductible contribution in a way that produces large taxable growth before conversion.
  • Assuming the strategy works the same way every year without checking current rules.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to convert right away?

Not required, but delaying can allow growth that becomes taxable on conversion. Many people convert promptly to keep the taxable portion small.

Does the backdoor work in a 401(k)?

The traditional IRA steps described here apply to IRAs. Some workplace plans allow other conversion methods, which have their own rules. Check your plan's documents.

Can my spouse do this too?

Yes, each spouse can use the strategy in their own IRA, subject to the rules that apply to them.
