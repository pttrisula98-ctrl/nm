HSA: The Only Triple Tax-Advantaged Account

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 9, 2026 · Updated November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

A Health Savings Account (HSA) is one of the few accounts in the US tax system that offers three advantages at once: contributions can reduce your taxable income, investment growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Used well, it can serve as a medical fund today and a long-term savings vehicle later.

The HSA is available only to people enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP), so eligibility is the first thing to check. This guide explains who qualifies, how the three tax benefits work, and how to decide whether to invest the balance or pay expenses out of pocket.

Who is eligible

To contribute to an HSA, you generally must be enrolled in an HDHP, must not have other disqualifying health coverage, and must not be claimed as a dependent on someone else's tax return. The HDHP must meet minimum deductible and out-of-pocket maximum requirements, which the IRS adjusts periodically. Enrollment mid-year can affect how much you may contribute, so check the rules that apply to your coverage start date.

The three tax advantages

Deductible contributions. Money you put into an HSA is generally deductible from your federal taxable income. If you contribute through payroll, the amount is usually excluded from wages, which can also reduce payroll taxes in many cases.

Tax-free growth. Interest, dividends, and capital gains earned inside the HSA are not taxed while they remain in the account. This matters most when you invest the balance rather than keeping it in cash.

Tax-free qualified withdrawals. Withdrawals used for qualified medical expenses are not taxed. Qualified expenses include a wide range of medical, dental, and vision costs, and the IRS publishes the current list.

Contribution limits

The IRS sets annual contribution limits that differ for individual and family coverage, and it allows an additional catch-up contribution for account holders aged 55 and older. The limits are adjusted periodically. Check the current IRS figures before contributing, because exceeding the limit can create tax penalties that must be corrected.

Investing inside an HSA

Many HSA providers let you invest part of your balance once it exceeds a set cash threshold. The available investments vary by provider, and fees, investment choices, and minimums differ widely. Compare these features before choosing a provider, especially if you plan to invest a significant balance over many years.

Pay now or save for later? If you can cover current medical costs from other savings, leaving the HSA invested lets it grow tax-free. Keep receipts for medical expenses you paid out of pocket, because you may reimburse yourself from the HSA years later, provided the expense was incurred after the HSA was established and you kept the documentation.

Using it as a retirement account

After age 65, HSA withdrawals for non-medical purposes are taxed as ordinary income but are not subject to the additional penalty that applies to early non-qualified withdrawals. This makes the HSA function similarly to a traditional IRA for non-medical spending in retirement, while still allowing tax-free use for medical costs. For this reason, some people invest their HSA for long-term growth and keep it separate from their current medical budget. Keep your own records of expenses so that tax-free reimbursement remains possible.

Common mistakes

  • Contributing without confirming HDHP eligibility for the months in question.
  • Exceeding the annual contribution limit, which can trigger penalties.
  • Leaving the balance in cash for years without considering whether investing fits your timeline.
  • Using HSA funds for non-qualified expenses before age 65, which can trigger tax and penalties.
  • Failing to keep receipts for medical expenses reimbursed later.

Frequently asked questions

Do I lose my HSA if I change jobs?

No. The HSA belongs to you. You can keep it, and you can continue to use it for qualified expenses, though you may need to stop contributing if you no longer have an HDHP.

Can I use my HSA for things other than medical care?

Withdrawals for non-medical purposes are taxable, and before age 65 they may also be subject to an additional penalty. After 65, the penalty no longer applies, but income tax still does.

Is an HSA better than a 401(k)?

They serve different purposes. An HSA offers unique tax treatment for medical costs. Many people use both if they qualify, and the right balance depends on their situation.
