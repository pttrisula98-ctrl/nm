Credit Utilization: The 30% Rule and How to Manage It

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026 · 6 min read

Credit utilization is the percentage of your available revolving credit that you are using at a given time. It is one of the most influential factors in many credit scores, and one of the easiest to adjust. Understanding when it is measured, not just how much you owe, is the key to managing it well.

How utilization is calculated

Utilization is generally your total revolving balances divided by your total revolving credit limits. If you have a $1,000 balance on a card with a $5,000 limit, that card's utilization is 20%. If you also have a second card with a $3,000 limit and no balance, your combined utilization is $1,000 divided by $8,000, or 12.5%. Scoring models look at both overall and per-card figures, so a single card near its limit can hurt your score even when your total usage is low.

The 30% guideline

A widely cited guideline is to keep utilization below 30%. It is a useful rule of thumb rather than a precise threshold, and many people with excellent scores use much less. Lower utilization is generally associated with stronger scores, so there is little reason to aim for the top of the range. If your utilization is above 30%, reducing it is often one of the fastest ways to improve your score.

Statement date versus due date

Many card issuers report your balance to the credit bureaus on or around your statement closing date, not your payment due date. That means the balance on your statement may be what gets reported, even if you pay in full later. If you want a lower reported balance, one approach is to pay down the card before the statement closes. Paying in full each month avoids interest, and paying before the statement date can also lower the balance that appears on your report. Check with your issuer to confirm when it reports.

Timing check: Find your statement closing date on your card account. If you want a lower balance reported, make a payment a few days before that date.

Paying in full versus carrying a balance

Some people believe that carrying a small balance from month to month helps their score. That is not required. Scoring models can see that you use the card and pay on time without paying interest. Paying your full statement balance each month avoids interest charges, keeps your payment history clean, and still shows active use. Carrying a balance only adds cost.

Why one percent may be better than zero

Some scoring models treat accounts with no recent reported activity less favorably than accounts with a small, regularly reported balance. This is why some people use a card for a small recurring purchase and pay it off in full. The benefit is modest and depends on the scoring model, so treat it as a minor refinement rather than a strategy. The core habit remains paying in full and on time.

Authorized user accounts

Being added as an authorized user on someone else's card can add that account's history and its credit limit to your report. If the primary account holder has a long history, low utilization, and perfect payments, the account can help your score. The reverse is also true: the primary holder's mistakes can appear on your report. Choose authorized user arrangements carefully, and check that the issuer reports authorized users to the bureaus before expecting a benefit.

Requesting a higher limit

Asking your issuer for a credit limit increase can reduce utilization without any change in spending, provided you do not increase your balance. Some issuers perform a hard inquiry for the request, and others do not. Ask before submitting a request, and avoid treating a higher limit as permission to spend more.

Common mistakes

  • Looking only at total utilization and ignoring a single maxed-out card.
  • Paying after the statement closes and assuming the high balance will not be reported.
  • Believing that carrying a balance is required to build credit.
  • Using a higher limit as an excuse to spend more, which raises utilization rather than lowering it.

Frequently asked questions

Does zero utilization hurt my score?

It can be less helpful than a small reported balance in some models, but it does not usually cause serious harm if you are otherwise managing credit well. The difference is generally small.

How fast will lowering my balance help?

Often after the next reporting cycle, which is typically within a month.

Does utilization affect every score the same way?

No. Different models weight it differently, and the same report can produce different scores across models.
