Roth Conversion Ladder: Early Access to Retirement Funds

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 12, 2026 · Updated November 12, 2026 · 8 min read

A Roth conversion ladder is a strategy for people who retire before the age at which they can withdraw from retirement accounts without penalty. Each year, you convert a portion of your traditional IRA to a Roth IRA and pay tax on the converted amount. After a waiting period, the converted money can be withdrawn without the early withdrawal penalty. Done carefully, the strategy can also smooth taxes across retirement.

Why the ladder exists

Withdrawals from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s before the penalty-free age generally trigger income tax plus an additional 10% penalty, with some exceptions. Roth IRA contributions can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, but earnings are subject to rules. The ladder uses conversions to move pre-tax money into the Roth, so that after the required waiting period, those converted amounts can be accessed without the penalty.

How it works

Each year, you convert a chosen amount from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. The converted amount is added to your taxable income for that year, and you pay tax on it. Five years later, that year's converted principal can be withdrawn without the 10% penalty. The process repeats each year, so that a new rung becomes accessible each year after the first five. The sequence is what gives the strategy its name.

YearActionWhat becomes accessible
Year 1Convert a set amount to RothNothing yet, five-year clock starts
Year 2Convert another amountNothing yet
Year 5Convert againYear 1 conversion, penalty-free
Year 6Convert againYear 2 conversion, penalty-free

These are the general mechanics. The exact timing and rules depend on your age, account types, and the way each conversion is tracked, so confirm the details before starting.

The five-year rule for converted amounts

Each conversion has its own five-year clock. The penalty on converted amounts withdrawn too early is generally the 10% additional tax, applied to the converted principal, not to growth that occurred in the Roth account. Keep detailed records of each conversion year, because the clocks run separately. Mixing up conversion years is one of the most common and costly errors in this strategy.

Track every rung: Record the amount and date of each conversion, and set a calendar reminder for the five-year anniversary. Without careful records, you may not know which dollars are penalty-free.

Managing tax brackets

The ladder works best when conversions are sized to fill a chosen tax bracket without pushing income into the next one. In early retirement, when wage income has stopped, taxable income may be low. Converting enough to use the room in a lower bracket can reduce lifetime taxes, particularly if future required distributions would otherwise push you into higher brackets. The right size depends on your other income, deductions, and state taxes, so model the outcome each year.

The ACA subsidy cliff

People who buy health insurance through marketplace plans may receive premium subsidies based on modified adjusted gross income. Conversions increase that income, which can reduce or eliminate subsidies, and the reduction can be steep once a threshold is crossed. This is sometimes called a subsidy cliff. For early retirees, the cost of losing subsidies can exceed the tax savings from a larger conversion, so coordinate conversions with health coverage planning. Subsidy rules and thresholds change, so check current federal guidance.

Risks and things to plan for

The ladder requires paying tax each year on conversions, which must be funded from non-retirement savings or other sources. Market declines after a conversion can leave you converting more shares than you would like in value terms, though the tax is based on the value converted. Rules and tax brackets can change. Keep a cash cushion for taxes and living expenses, and review the plan annually.

Common mistakes

  • Withdrawing a converted amount before its five-year clock ends and paying the penalty.
  • Converting too much in one year and moving into an unexpectedly high bracket.
  • Ignoring ACA subsidy effects on marketplace health coverage.
  • Not having funds to pay the conversion tax, which can force a sale at a bad time.
  • Losing track of which conversion year each withdrawal belongs to.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a penalty on conversions themselves?

Conversions are not penalized in the same way as early withdrawals, but the converted amount is taxable as income in the year of conversion.

Can I use this for a 401(k)?

Often a 401(k) can be rolled to an IRA first, then converted. Plan rules and rollover timing matter, so check with your plan administrator.

Do I need a professional?

Many people benefit from modeling the strategy with a tax professional, given the interaction between conversions, brackets, and healthcare subsidies.
