Mega Backdoor Roth: After-Tax 401(k) Contributions Explained

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published October 30, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026 · 8 min read

The mega backdoor Roth is a strategy that lets some workers save far more in Roth-style accounts than the usual IRA limits allow. It works by making after-tax contributions to a workplace 401(k), then converting or moving those contributions into a Roth account. It is available only if your plan allows the right features, and it requires careful execution.

Why it matters

The standard employee contribution limit for a 401(k) and the separate IRA limit both cap how much you can save in tax-advantaged accounts each year. There is also an overall annual limit that covers employee contributions, employer contributions, and certain after-tax contributions together. If your total contributions are below that overall limit after you have maxed out the standard employee deferral, the space in between can be used for after-tax contributions. This space can be substantial, which is why the strategy attracts attention.

How it works

The strategy has three basic steps. First, you contribute to your 401(k) beyond the standard employee deferral limit, using after-tax dollars. These contributions are not deductible and have no immediate tax benefit. Second, you move the after-tax contributions into a Roth account, either inside the plan or through a distribution to a Roth IRA. Third, future growth on the Roth portion is tax-free if it is qualified.

In-plan conversion versus in-service distribution

There are two main ways to move after-tax 401(k) money into a Roth account. An in-plan Roth conversion converts the after-tax contributions to a Roth 401(k) within the same plan, if the plan allows it. An in-service distribution moves the after-tax money out of the plan to a Roth IRA while you are still employed, if your plan permits that type of withdrawal.

The main difference is speed and tax exposure. Converting quickly after contributing keeps taxable growth small, because only earnings that accumulate before conversion are taxed. Delays can leave more growth exposed to tax, and the handling of earnings can differ between the two methods. Plans also differ in how they handle the pre-tax and after-tax portions of a single distribution, so check the rules carefully.

Speed matters: The sooner after-tax contributions are converted or moved, the less taxable growth they accumulate. Find out how quickly your plan processes conversions.

Plan requirements

The strategy depends on what your employer's plan allows. Many plans do not permit after-tax contributions at all. Others allow them but do not offer in-plan conversions or in-service distributions of after-tax money. Some plans offer one feature but not the other. Before planning around the strategy, ask your plan administrator for the specific provisions, including contribution limits, conversion rules, and any restrictions on withdrawals.

Limits and timing

The overall annual limit on total contributions is set by the IRS and adjusted periodically. Your employer's match and other contributions count toward it, so generous matches reduce the room available for after-tax savings. Calculate the space available each year, and remember that changes in pay or employer contributions can alter it. Coordinate with the timing of your payroll deductions, because some plans apply limits based on the calendar year.

Who may benefit

The strategy can be valuable for higher earners who have already contributed the standard pre-tax maximum, want more tax-free savings, and have a plan that supports the mechanics. It is less useful if your plan does not allow the features, if you have limited cash flow, or if you need the money before retirement. Compare it with other options, including a Roth IRA where eligible, a backdoor Roth IRA, and an HSA where available.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming every 401(k) plan allows after-tax contributions or conversions.
  • Letting after-tax money sit for a long period, which exposes the growth to tax.
  • Forgetting to count employer contributions toward the overall annual limit.
  • Mixing pre-tax and after-tax amounts when requesting a distribution.
  • Skipping the plan's written rules and relying on secondhand descriptions.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as a backdoor Roth IRA?

They share a similar goal, but they use different accounts and limits. The backdoor Roth uses IRAs, while the mega backdoor uses 401(k) plans with after-tax contributions.

Does the strategy create taxes?

Earnings on after-tax contributions are generally taxable when they are converted or distributed, and any pre-tax amounts are taxed as well. Check the specific rules that apply.

Should I ask my employer to add this feature?

Some workers request plan changes. Your plan administrator can tell you what options are available and whether changes are feasible.
