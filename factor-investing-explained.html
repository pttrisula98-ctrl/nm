Factor Investing: Value, Momentum, Quality, Size – Explained

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published October 25, 2026 · Updated October 25, 2026 · 9 min read

Factor investing is an approach that tilts a portfolio toward characteristics that academic research has linked to higher long-term returns, such as low valuations, strong recent performance, profitability, or small company size. It sits between plain index investing and active stock picking, and it has become popular with investors who want more control over their exposures.

The approach is more nuanced than its marketing sometimes suggests. The evidence for some factors is strong, the evidence for others is weaker, and every factor has long periods of underperformance. This guide explains what the main factors are, what the research shows, and how to think about whether they belong in your portfolio.

What a factor is

A factor is a characteristic that explains differences in returns across stocks. Broad market exposure, measured by a market index, is the most basic factor. Academic researchers have identified additional characteristics that appear to be associated with returns over long periods. Using these characteristics, a fund can overweight stocks that have them and underweight those that do not.

The main factors

Value. Value stocks trade at low prices relative to measures such as earnings, book value, or cash flow. Investors who buy them are betting that the market has underpriced their future prospects. Value has a long academic history, but it has underperformed for extended periods, including much of the decade before the time of writing.

Momentum. Momentum stocks have performed well over the recent past, typically over several months to a year. The idea is that trends persist. Momentum can produce strong returns, but it can also reverse sharply, and portfolios tilted toward it may need to trade more often.

Quality. Quality stocks are associated with strong profitability, stable earnings, and low debt. Investors often see quality as a defensive characteristic, though the evidence and definitions vary across studies and funds.

Size. Small-company stocks have historically returned more than large ones in many studies, although the size premium has been inconsistent over time and has been debated. Smaller companies can also be less liquid and more volatile.

What the evidence suggests

Academic research, including influential studies from the 1990s onward, has documented several factors across many markets and time periods. This body of work is one reason factor investing has gained attention. However, researchers continue to debate whether these premiums reflect compensation for risk, behavioral mistakes, or patterns that may weaken once they are widely known. Some factor premiums have shrunk after publication, and the performance of any single factor can vary widely from one decade to the next.

The honest summary is that factors have shown promise, but they do not guarantee higher returns in any given period. Anyone considering a factor strategy should expect years of underperformance relative to a broad index.

Expect long stretches of lag: A factor tilt that trails the market for several years is not necessarily broken. It may simply be out of favor. Decide in advance whether you can hold through that period.

Factor funds and tilts

Factor investing can be implemented through funds that target one or more factors, often called tilt funds. Some of these are index-based and rules-driven, while others are actively managed. Compared with a broad index fund, factor funds typically charge higher expense ratios and can produce a different return pattern, sometimes with greater tracking differences from the broad market.

Multi-factor funds combine several characteristics to diversify across factors. They can smooth out some of the variability of single-factor exposure, though they still carry the risk that the factors underperform together.

Should factors be in your core portfolio?

For most investors, a broad market index portfolio is the core, and factor tilts are optional additions. If you add them, consider a modest allocation that you can hold through lean periods, keep costs in view, and avoid switching factors based on recent results. Factor tilts can complicate a simple portfolio, and they are not necessary to build a diversified, low-cost portfolio. Our guide on the three-fund portfolio shows a simpler starting point.

Common mistakes

  • Chasing the factor that performed best over the last few years.
  • Assuming factor premiums are guaranteed.
  • Paying high fees for a factor exposure that could be obtained more cheaply.
  • Adding several overlapping factor funds that effectively double up on the same tilt.

Frequently asked questions

Is factor investing the same as active management?

Not necessarily. Many factor funds follow transparent rules rather than manager judgment. Some are actively managed, so it depends on the specific product.

Which factor is the best?

No factor is best in all periods. Each has produced strong and weak stretches. Diversification across factors is one way to reduce reliance on any single one.

Do I need factor funds to succeed?

No. A low-cost, diversified index portfolio is a sound foundation for many investors, and factor funds are an optional refinement.
