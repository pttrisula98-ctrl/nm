Asset Allocation: Stocks vs Bonds by Age and Risk Tolerance

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 7, 2026 · Updated November 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Asset allocation is the split between stocks, bonds, and cash in your portfolio. It affects your expected return and the size of the swings you will experience more than almost any other decision. Many people look for a simple formula based on age, and such formulas can be a starting point, but they leave out the factors that matter most.

This guide explains common rules of thumb, how glide paths work, and why your ability and willingness to absorb losses should guide the final choice more than your birth year.

What stocks and bonds do in a portfolio

Stocks represent ownership in companies. Over long periods they have historically delivered higher returns than bonds, but they can fall sharply, sometimes by a third or more in a severe downturn. Bonds are loans to governments or companies. They usually pay steady interest and tend to fluctuate less than stocks, though they can still lose value, particularly when interest rates rise. Cash and cash equivalents are the most stable, but they typically offer the lowest long-term growth and can lose purchasing power to inflation.

The mix matters because stocks and bonds often respond differently to economic events. Bonds can cushion the impact of a stock decline, which can help you stay invested. That benefit is the main reason most diversified portfolios hold both.

Common rules of thumb

One popular heuristic subtracts your age from a fixed number, such as 100 or 110, and assigns the result to stocks, with the rest in bonds. A 35-year-old using 110 would hold 75% stocks and 25% bonds. Another rule of thumb suggests holding a bond percentage equal to your age. These formulas are easy to remember, and they reflect a real principle: as your time horizon shortens, you have less time to recover from losses, so a more conservative mix usually makes sense.

The limitation is that a formula cannot see your situation. Two people of the same age can have very different incomes, savings, pensions, debts, and comfort with volatility. A rule that fits one may be too aggressive or too cautious for the other.

Glide paths

A glide path is a planned schedule for gradually shifting from stocks to bonds over time. Target-date retirement funds use this approach, reducing their stock share as the target date approaches. A glide path can make the transition automatic and reduce the temptation to time changes. Whether you follow one or design your own, the principle is the same: reduce risk as the money becomes more likely to be needed.

Capacity and willingness to take risk

Two concepts are worth separating. Risk capacity is your financial ability to absorb losses without changing your life plans. It depends on your income stability, savings, debts, dependents, and how long before you need the money. Risk willingness is your emotional tolerance for seeing your balance fall. An allocation that is too aggressive for your willingness can lead you to sell during a downturn, locking in losses. An allocation that is too conservative for your capacity can leave you short of long-term goals.

The right allocation is usually where the two overlap. If your capacity is high but your willingness is low, you may need to accept a more moderate mix than the numbers suggest, and focus on staying invested through the ups and downs.

Test your tolerance: Imagine your portfolio dropping 30% in a year. Ask yourself whether you would hold, buy more, or sell. Your honest answer is a better guide than any formula.

Revisit the mix

Life changes, and so should your allocation. A new job, a new child, a home purchase, an inheritance, or a shift toward retirement can all change your capacity for risk. Review your allocation at least once a year, and when your circumstances change. Rebalancing back to your target keeps the mix consistent with your plan.

Common mistakes

  • Following an age formula without considering income stability or other resources.
  • Choosing an allocation during a market rally and abandoning it during a decline.
  • Holding too much cash for long-term goals, which can lose purchasing power over time.
  • Ignoring other assets, such as a pension or home equity, when deciding how much to hold in portfolio investments.

Frequently asked questions

Is the "110 minus age" rule correct?

It is a heuristic, not a law. It can be a reasonable starting point for discussion, but it should be adjusted for your specific circumstances.

Should I hold bonds if I am young?

Some young investors hold a small bond allocation for diversification or to reduce volatility. Others choose mostly stocks. The right choice depends on your capacity and willingness to take risk.

What if I want to avoid losses entirely?

Stable, low-risk assets can protect principal, but they may not keep pace with inflation over long periods. Understand the trade-off before choosing a conservative mix.
