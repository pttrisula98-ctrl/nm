Credit Freeze vs Fraud Alert vs Lock: Which to Use When

By Money Mechanics Editorial Team · Published November 4, 2026 · Updated November 4, 2026 · 5 min read

If your personal information has been exposed in a breach, or you suspect someone is trying to open accounts in your name, you have several tools to slow them down. The three main options in the US are a credit freeze, a fraud alert, and a credit lock. They work differently, and they fit different situations.

Credit freeze

A credit freeze restricts access to your credit report. Most lenders check your report before approving a new account, so a freeze generally stops new credit from being opened in your name until you lift it. A freeze is free under US federal law and must be placed separately with each of the three nationwide consumer reporting agencies: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. It does not affect your existing accounts, your credit score, or your ability to use cards you already have.

To apply for credit yourself, you must temporarily lift the freeze, usually for a specific period or a specific lender. Lifting is also free. Keep track of the PINs or passwords you receive, because you will need them to lift or remove the freeze.

Fraud alert

A fraud alert asks lenders to take extra steps to verify your identity before opening new credit. An initial fraud alert lasts one year and can be renewed. If you have been a victim of identity theft and provide an identity theft report, you can request an extended fraud alert, which typically lasts seven years. A fraud alert placed with one bureau is shared with the other two. Fraud alerts are free.

Unlike a freeze, a fraud alert does not block credit. Lenders are asked to verify your identity, but they can still approve applications. That makes it a lighter tool.

Credit lock

A credit lock is a service typically offered by a credit bureau or a private company. It lets you turn credit access on and off through an app or website. Locks are often marketed as a convenient alternative to freezes. They are not governed by the same federal rules as freezes, and their terms, features, and fees vary. Read the service agreement carefully, and confirm what it covers before relying on it.

Which to use when

If you have no immediate plans to apply for credit and want strong protection, a credit freeze at all three bureaus is usually the most effective free option. If you are applying for credit regularly and want a lighter step, a fraud alert may be enough. If you have already been a victim, file an identity theft report with the FTC, and consider an extended fraud alert along with a freeze. A credit lock can be useful for convenience, but make sure it provides the protection you need.

Freeze all three: A freeze at one bureau does not protect your files at the other two. Place the freeze with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion separately.

Protecting a child's credit

Children's Social Security numbers can be misused, and the fraud may go unnoticed for years. Many parents place a freeze on a child's credit file. Some bureaus allow you to request this for a minor, usually with proof of your relationship and the child's information. The process differs by bureau, so check each bureau's instructions. A freeze can be lifted later when the child needs credit.

Keep records and monitor

Save any confirmation letters, PINs, and reference numbers. Check your credit reports regularly. You are entitled to free reports from each of the three bureaus through the official government site, and you can monitor activity between reports. If you see accounts you do not recognize, contact the lender and the bureaus, and consider reporting the activity to the FTC.

Common mistakes

  • Freezing only one bureau and assuming the others are protected.
  • Forgetting a freeze is in place and then being unable to apply for credit.
  • Losing freeze PINs or passwords and having to go through extra verification.
  • Paying for a service that duplicates what a free freeze already provides.

Frequently asked questions

Does a freeze hurt my credit score?

No. A freeze does not change your score. It only limits lenders' access to your report for new applications.

Can a freeze stop existing accounts from being charged?

No. A freeze affects new credit. It does not prevent fraud on existing cards, so monitor those accounts directly.

How long does lifting a freeze take?

Timing depends on the bureau and the method you use. Plan ahead, especially if you have a deadline for an application.
